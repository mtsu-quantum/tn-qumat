## Three Research Thrusts

TN-QuMat research and training are organized around three thrusts, each of which was represented in the workshop program.

- **Thrust 1: Many-body theory of disordered correlated quantum materials.** How disorder and strong correlations shape phenomena such as the Mott metal-insulator transition and spin and charge order in high-temperature superconductors, studied with quantum embedding methods and DCA++ on ORNL’s leadership-class supercomputers.
- **Thrust 2: Ab initio study of topologically nontrivial quantum materials.** First-principles studies of topological Anderson insulators and quantum spin liquids, using density functional theory and Wannier function-based modeling to connect theory with experiment.
- **Thrust 3: Synthesis and characterization of low-dimensional quantum systems.** Growth and analysis of thin-film topological materials and their heterostructures, including how substrates, stoichiometry, and controlled disorder affect their optical and electronic properties.

## Hands-on Training: From Model Hamiltonians to Quantum Algorithms

The afternoon translated the morning’s lectures into practice. Eighty registrants signed up for two rounds of parallel sessions in TSU’s computer laboratories, with Zoom access for remote participants. About 40 percent of registrants reported no prior familiarity with elementary Unix commands, and the sessions took participants from first steps to research-level calculations with the tools used in the field.

- **Many-body methods (ALPS, DCA++).** Led by Hanna Terletska, Anirudha Mirmira (MTSU), and Fei Lin (Virginia Tech). Participants built Hamiltonians step by step in Python, from the one-dimensional tight-binding chain to the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model and the Anderson model of random disorder. They computed energy levels, obtained the density of states from the Green function, and connected these results to the disorder-driven metal-insulator transition. Lin then introduced ALPS through dynamical mean-field theory of the Hubbard model on a Bethe lattice, where the spin-resolved Green functions distinguish the high-temperature paramagnetic state from the low-temperature antiferromagnetic state.
- **Density functional theory.** Led by Jack Villanova (MTSU). Using Quantum ESPRESSO and VESTA on a TN-QuMat virtual machine, participants studied the stacking of α-RuCl<sub>3</sub>, a Kitaev quantum spin liquid candidate. They visualized crystal structures, ran total-energy calculations for different stacking shifts, and pooled their results to identify the favored stacking. They then compared their findings with fully relaxed DFT+U calculations including spin-orbit coupling and van der Waals corrections, in which the rhombohedral stacking has the lowest energy, followed by C2/m. A further exercise treated the O atom, the O<sup>+</sup> ion, and the O<sub>2</sub> molecule.
- **Quantum computing.** Led by Daniel Nino of Xanadu and Ryan Harring of IonQ. Working in PennyLane, Xanadu’s open-source software library, participants first built an entangled Bell-state circuit. They then ran the variational quantum eigensolver for the linear H<sub>3</sub><sup>+</sup> molecule, using PySCF to prepare Hartree-Fock, CISD, and CCSD initial states and comparing how each affects convergence to the ground-state energy.
- **AI for materials.** Mariia Karabin and Wasim Mondal (MTSU) led a detailed hands-on training that followed directly from Heinz’s talk. After surveying the materials properties that machine learning can predict, participants focused on the formation energy, a measure of compound stability, using a calculated dataset of about 420 Heusler alloys in the L2<sub>1</sub> and XA structures.

The day closed with a tour of experimental facilities at TSU’s TIGER Institute, led by Richard Mu, linking the computational training to materials synthesis and characterization in the laboratory.

## Reach Across Tennessee and Beyond

About 100 participants took part in the workshop, reflecting strong demand for quantum training across institutions and disciplines. Registrants included 32 undergraduate students, 30 graduate students, 4 postdoctoral researchers, and 28 faculty members, from fields including physics, computer and data science, chemistry, mathematics, and engineering.

Forty-one registrants (40 percent) came from the partner HBCUs, TSU, Fisk, and Meharry, and 29 from MTSU. Participants also came from across the country and abroad, including the University of Tennessee, Knoxville, Vanderbilt University, the University of Colorado Boulder, Virginia Tech, Hawassa University in Ethiopia, and JNCASR in India, together with the quantum companies IonQ and Xanadu. More than 20 institutions were represented in all. This response underscores the need for accessible quantum training for undergraduate and graduate students across fields, well beyond physics.
